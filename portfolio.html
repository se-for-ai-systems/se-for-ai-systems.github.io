Portfolio & Exam

Software Engineering for AI Systems

Overview

The portfolio is built around a problem from your own company. Each portfolio assignment applies one seminar theme to your case: P1 software architecture, P2 the cloud continuum, P3 big data & data science technologies. It is introduced at that seminar, handed in before the next one, and reviewed by two peers. The three assignments, revised based on the feedback, form your final exam report.

Each week, the shared reference project (HydraWatch, a fictional hydraulic condition-monitoring company) shows the techniques, and you then apply them to your own case.

Templates

Write each assignment in its LaTeX template. Each template has one section per deliverable, with tables for the scenarios, ADRs, data placement and governance. The assessment criteria are in a box at the top; delete it before submitting. The .pdf shows what the template looks like.

  • P1 – Software Architecture — .zip / .pdf
  • P2 – Cloud Continuum — .zip / .pdf
  • P3 – Big Data & Data Science Technologies — .zip / .pdf
  • Final report — .zip / .pdf

How to use a template:

  1. Overleaf: New Project → Upload Project → choose the .zip. Locally: unzip it and run latexmk -pdf main.tex (needs TeX Live, MacTeX or MiKTeX).
  2. Set \reportsubtitle{Case: ...} to a short, anonymised case name, and \author{...} to your name. For a group, separate the names with \and.
  3. Replace the guidance text in each section and the ... in the tables. Number scenarios (QA-1, ...) and ADRs (ADR-1, ...) across all three portfolios, so P2 and P3 can refer back to P1.
  4. Submit the compiled PDF.

Each zip also has a README.md with these steps.

Your Case

What makes a suitable case

  1. An AI component, existing or planned. A model in a notebook is enough; LLM-based cases are welcome.
  2. A deployment context with at least two places to run, e.g. device, plant, office server and cloud.
  3. Data that keeps arriving, e.g. sensor readings, transactions, logs or documents.
  4. At least one constraint that matters, e.g. latency, availability, privacy, data sovereignty or cost.

You pitch your case at Seminar 1 (3 minutes: context, users, data, why ML, the constraint that matters). A sanitised pitch is fine – no company names are needed. The course responsible approves the case by Wed 3 Feb, or points you to the fallback case.

Confidentiality

  • Describe your system at the level of components and data types, and anonymise names where needed.
  • Company code, data and model weights never go on the course cluster, into a public container registry or into a public repository. Use synthetic or public data with the same shape instead. Other students can read the resources in your namespace.
  • Peer reviewers and examiners treat submissions as confidential.
  • If your company cannot share the case, or has no suitable problem, use the HydraWatch reference case with the same deliverables.

Evidence

Analysis and design always concern your own case. Hands-on evidence (running deployments, pipelines, dashboards) may come from the reference project where your own case cannot run on public or synthetic data.

Groups

You work individually by default. Small groups (at most 3 students) share one case and one report. The oral exam is always individual, covers the whole report, and the grade is individual.

P1 – Software Architecture

Turn your company problem into measurable requirements and an architecture for the AI system.

Guiding questions

  • Who are the stakeholders, and what is the business goal?
  • Which quality attributes matter most, and which conflict? What does a wrong prediction cost?
  • Which parts are AI and which are not, and how do they interact?
  • Which existing systems (e.g. ERP, historian, PLC/SCADA, data platform) must it integrate with, and across which network zones?
  • Which risk category of the EU AI Act does the system fall into, and what follows from it?
  • Which technical debt will the design accumulate?

Deliverables

  • Problem and context: business goal, stakeholders, data available, why ML is needed.
  • 3–5 quality-attribute scenarios (source, stimulus, environment, artefact, response, measure), including at least one model-quality scenario (e.g. recall on a time-based hold-out set, with the cost of a missed case stated).
  • C4 context and container diagrams, with the AI and non-AI parts marked.
  • At least two architecture decision records (ADRs) with the alternatives considered.
  • EU AI Act risk classification of the system, with a short justification.
  • Technical-debt risks (cf. Sculley et al.) and how the design limits them.
  • If your case uses an LLM: how prompts and model versions are managed, how outputs are evaluated, and which guardrails apply.

Assessment criteria

  • Scenarios are measurable and traceable to the business goal.
  • Diagrams are consistent with the scenarios and the ADRs.
  • ADRs name real alternatives and the trade-off that decided them.
  • Debt risks and the AI Act classification are specific to the case, not a generic list.

P2 – Cloud Continuum

Decide where each part of your system runs, and how it is secured, deployed and kept reliable.

Guiding questions

  • Which component runs where (edge, on-premises, cloud), and why?
  • Which data may leave which tier, and which rule says so (contract, GDPR, customer policy)?
  • How is the infrastructure secured: network boundaries, secrets, least privilege?
  • How does heterogeneous compute (small devices, CPUs, GPUs) constrain the choice of model?
  • Build or buy: a managed cloud or vendor ML platform, or self-hosted? What does each cost, and what is the lock-in?
  • What happens when a part fails or the connection drops?

Deliverables

  • Deployment view: which component runs on which tier, traced to the P1 scenarios.
  • Data-placement table: each data item, where it may be stored and processed, and the rule behind it.
  • An ADR comparing at least one managed or vendor option with self-hosting (cost, lock-in, skills, operations).
  • Security and reliability tactics, mapped to the components they protect.
  • Kubernetes manifests or Helm chart, CI and deployment workflow, and how data and models are versioned (own case or reference project).
  • Evidence: repository (a private repository or a zip upload is fine), kubectl get all, and a deleted pod or failed model showing recovery.

Assessment criteria

  • Placement decisions follow from the P1 quality attributes and constraints.
  • Data flows respect the stated sovereignty rules, and the design enforces them.
  • At least one security and one reliability tactic are demonstrated, not only described.
  • A model can be rebuilt from Git, DVC and an MLflow run id, and one command deploys the system.

P3 – Big Data & Data Science Technologies

Design how data flows through your system, how it is stored and governed, and how changes in the data are noticed.

Guiding questions

  • What data does the system produce and consume, at what volume and rate?
  • Batch, stream or both – and why?
  • Where are historical and event data stored (topic retention, data lake, warehouse, feature store)?
  • Who owns which data, how long is it kept, and which regulations apply (e.g. GDPR, NIS2, EU AI Act) – and what do they require of the design?
  • How is data quality checked, and how is drift detected and acted on while labels arrive late?

Deliverables

  • Data-flow diagram from source to storage to model, with the tier boundaries from P2.
  • Volume and rate estimate, with the calculation shown.
  • Processing choice (batch, stream or both) and storage choice, each with an ADR.
  • Governance table: data item, owner, retention, applicable rule, location.
  • Observability design: data-quality checks, drift and proxy metrics, service SLOs vs. model-quality metrics, and the feedback loop (retrain, roll back or reconfigure).
  • Evidence of a running pipeline. Dashboards and drift evidence from week 6 may be added in the final report.

Assessment criteria

  • The volume estimate is calculated and drives the processing and storage choices.
  • Governance rules are concrete and consistent with the P2 data-placement table.
  • Drift and quality signals are defined, measurable and tied to an action.

Peer Review

Each submission is reviewed by two peers before the next seminar. Use the assessment criteria of the assignment, and answer three questions:

  1. Which criterion is met best, and why?
  2. Which criterion is met least, and what is the biggest risk or weakness in the design?
  3. One question you would ask the author at the exam.

Final Report

  • A one-page recommendation for a non-technical decision maker: options, cost, risks, and what you recommend.
  • Short introduction to the company problem.
  • P1, P2 and P3, revised based on peer and lecturer feedback.
  • Reflection: what changed and why, and what you would do next in a real production setting.
  • Page limit: 15–20 pages.
